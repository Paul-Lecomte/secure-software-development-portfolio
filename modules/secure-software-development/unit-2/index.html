Assignment 1: Blog Post — Managing People to Reduce Insider Cybersecurity Threats

Blog Post — Individual Assignment

People are often considered the weakest link in cybersecurity. However, insider threats can result from malicious actions, human error or inadequate training. Organisations should therefore combine technical safeguards with employee awareness and a positive security culture.

Five concepts from ISO/IEC 27000 help explain how people can be managed to reduce these threats (ISO/IEC, 2026).

Information security

Information security involves protecting information and ensuring its confidentiality, integrity and availability. Clear policies and regular staff training help employees understand their responsibilities.

Confidentiality

Confidentiality means preventing unauthorised disclosure of information. Role-based access control and multifactor authentication can reduce the risk of employees accessing or sharing sensitive data inappropriately.

Integrity

Integrity involves protecting information from unauthorised alteration. Audit trails, separation of duties and regular reviews can help identify suspicious changes and prevent misuse.

Risk

Risk concerns uncertainty that may affect organisational objectives. Managers should assess insider threats by considering their likelihood, potential impact and the level of access employees hold.

Risk treatment

Risk treatment involves selecting appropriate measures to address identified risks. Organisations should apply least-privilege access, remove permissions promptly when employees leave, provide security awareness training and establish clear procedures for reporting incidents. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA, 2020) also emphasises the importance of a coordinated approach to insider threat mitigation.

Balance is key. However, excessive monitoring can damage employee trust. Security measures should be proportionate, transparent and respectful of privacy.

Ultimately, employees should not simply be viewed as security risks. With appropriate training, clear responsibilities and effective safeguards, people can become an important part of an organisation’s defence against insider attacks.

References

  • Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) (2020) Insider threat mitigation guide. Washington, DC: CISA.
  • International Organization for Standardization and International Electrotechnical Commission (ISO/IEC) (2026) ISO/IEC 27000:2026 Information security, cybersecurity and privacy protection: Information security management systems: Overview. Geneva: ISO.

Assignment 2: Peer Critique Responses

Two critique responses to peer assignments

Critique Response 1

Peer assignment reviewed: IoT Healthcare: Misuse Case Analysis

I like how your misuse case diagram highlights realistic threats to remote patient monitoring, particularly data tampering and denial-of-service attacks. You clearly explain how these threats can affect patient privacy, clinical decisions and patient safety. Your proposed controls, including encryption, multifactor authentication and device security, are also relevant to protecting healthcare IoT systems (Li et al., 2024; Fagan et al., 2020).

One weakness is the focus on external attackers. A malicious insider, such as an employee misusing authorised access or accidentally exposing patient data, could also compromise the system. Adding an insider actor would make the diagram more comprehensive. It would also help to distinguish threats to confidentiality, integrity and availability so their different impacts are clearer.

The diagram could be improved by linking each misuse case directly to its countermeasure. For example, integrity verification could address data tampering, while resilient infrastructure and local alerts could reduce the impact of denial-of-service attacks. This would make the relationship between threats and safeguards easier to understand.

Overall, your post provides a strong foundation for security-by-design. How would you balance stronger security controls with the limited battery life and processing capacity of wearable devices?

References

  • Fagan, M., Megas, K.N., Scarfone, K. and Smith, M. (2020) IoT device cybersecurity capability core baseline. NISTIR 8259A. Gaithersburg, MD: National Institute of Standards and Technology. Available at: https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.IR.8259A.
  • Li, C. et al. (2024) ‘A review of IoT applications in healthcare’, Neurocomputing, 565, 127017. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neucom.2023.127017.

Critique Response 2

Peer assignment reviewed: Smart University: Misuse Case Analysis

I like how your diagram connects specific smart university functions, such as Wi-Fi access, attendance monitoring and CCTV, to realistic misuse cases. Including both an external attacker and a malicious insider is particularly effective because each presents different risks. The use of threat and mitigation relationships also makes the security requirements easier to follow (Sindre and Opdahl, 2005).

One improvement would be to make the phishing mitigation more specific. Your narrative identifies data exfiltration as the countermeasure, but this is actually a possible consequence of a successful phishing attack. The diagram could instead show multifactor authentication to reduce the risk of stolen credentials being misused, alongside email filtering and staff awareness training.

I would also suggest expanding the attendance spoofing misuse case. For example, an attacker could manipulate sensor data or use a cloned smart card to register attendance fraudulently. Linking this threat to stronger card authentication and sensor-data integrity checks would make the proposed controls clearer. Finally, adding the denial-of-service attack you mention would show how disruption to the VLE or campus Wi-Fi could affect students and staff.

Overall, your diagram is well structured and relevant to a smart university environment. Which threat would you prioritise first: compromised student credentials, attendance manipulation or CCTV hijacking, and why?

References

  • Sindre, G. and Opdahl, A.L. (2005) ‘Eliciting security requirements with misuse cases’, Requirements Engineering, 10(1), pp. 34–44. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00766-004-0194-4.
